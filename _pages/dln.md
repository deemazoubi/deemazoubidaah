# Digital Literacy Narrative	

## ChatGPT
As a 19-year-old Economics student at NYU Abu Dhabi with a minor in Interactive Media, I have come to realize that digital literacy is not simply about knowing how to use technology. It also involves understanding how digital tools shape the way we create, communicate, research, and interpret information. Before taking this course, I mainly associated my digital skills with the tools I regularly used for academic work and creative projects. However, I have gradually started to see digital literacy as something much broader, particularly through the different ways we have explored how computers process and represent information.
For content creation, I regularly use tools such as Canva and social media platforms. Canva has become one of the tools I am most comfortable with because I use it to create presentations, posters, social media content, and other visual materials. I also enjoy designing and editing content, which has made me more aware of how visual choices influence communication and how information is received by different audiences. Academically, my digital skills are also connected to research and data analysis. As an Economics student, I regularly work with quantitative information and have been learning to use R and RStudio, alongside Excel and spreadsheets, to organize, analyze, and interpret data. I previously thought of these creative and analytical skills as relatively separate, but this course has encouraged me to recognize how they can complement one another.
One of the first ideas that challenged my understanding of digital research was the distinction between close reading and distant reading. I was familiar with reading individual texts and interpreting their meaning, but I had not really considered how computational tools could help examine patterns across much larger collections of texts. Through experimenting with tools such as Voyant and working with computational notebooks, I was introduced to methods such as word frequency analysis and identifying distinctive words across different texts. Although I only explored some of these techniques, I found it interesting that computers could help identify patterns that might be difficult to notice through reading individual documents. At the same time, I began to understand that identifying a frequently used word does not automatically explain its meaning or significance. A computer can reveal a pattern, but interpreting why that pattern exists still requires human judgment. This changed how I think about the relationship between computational analysis and traditional research. Rather than replacing close reading, distant reading can complement it by helping researchers identify broader patterns and then return to individual texts with new questions.
Another important development in my understanding came from learning how digital data is created in the first place. Before this course, I often thought of datasets as information that already existed in an organized format and was simply waiting to be analyzed. However, our work with historical documents introduced me to a different process. We explored technologies such as OCR and automated text recognition, as well as how machines can be trained to read complex documents. This made me realize that transforming historical material into digital information involves much more than scanning a document. Computers have to identify and interpret what they see, and this process is not always accurate.
I experienced this more directly through our project using Lorimer's Gazetteer. We worked with historical entries containing information about settlements, tribes, and the number of houses associated with different groups. Using AI to help extract information from these documents made the process more manageable, but it also made me aware of the importance of checking the information rather than automatically accepting what a model produces. Historical names, numbers, and unfamiliar words can be interpreted incorrectly, meaning that errors can become part of a dataset if they are not identified. We then had to organize the extracted information into a structured table, clean the data, and match settlements with geographical coordinates. This process made me appreciate how much work takes place before data can actually be analyzed. Broman and Woo's reading, Data Organizations in Spreadsheets, helped me connect this experience to the importance of organizing data consistently. The way information is structured is not just a technical decision; it affects how easily it can be interpreted and what kinds of analysis become possible.
Working with geographical data also introduced me to a different way of thinking about information. Using Kepler.gl, we were able to turn our historical dataset into a map, making geographical relationships and patterns more visible. Previously, I mainly thought of maps as ways of showing where places are located. However, I started to recognize that maps are also forms of representation. Decisions about which information to include, how to categorize it, and which symbols to use can influence what someone notices. A map can make certain relationships immediately visible while leaving other aspects of the historical context less apparent. This connects to the idea of thick mapping, which encourages us to look beyond geographical locations and consider the social, cultural, and historical information connected to them. The Hypercities reading on Thick Mapping, helped me think about maps as more than visual displays of coordinates. It made me consider how spatial visualizations can communicate richer stories about places and the people connected to them.
More recently, our exploration of images introduced another dimension to my understanding of digital literacy. I found it particularly interesting to learn about how computers are taught to recognize and interpret pictures. Through discussions and practical exercises involving computer vision, image recognition, and distant viewing, I began to question what it actually means for a computer to understand an image. We explored how computational methods can examine visual material and identify patterns, but also how images can sometimes be misinterpreted by these systems. In one exercise involving manipulated images, we saw how something that appeared recognizable to a person, such as a broom, could be classified as something entirely different by a computer. This made me think about the limitations of machine recognition. Humans interpret images using context and experiences, whereas computer systems rely on computational processes and patterns learned from data. Distant viewing was particularly interesting because it extended the idea of distant reading to visual material, allowing us to examine larger collections of images rather than focusing on individual pictures. As someone who enjoys creating visual content, I found this especially relevant. Designing an image for a human audience and understanding how a computer processes that same image are two very different things.
These experiences have also changed how I think about artificial intelligence. I already used AI tools for brainstorming, explaining difficult concepts, and exploring ideas, but I now see more clearly that their usefulness depends on how critically they are approached. Our work with historical documents showed me that AI can make research processes more efficient, but it cannot remove the need for verification and interpretation. Even when an output looks convincing, it may contain mistakes or overlook important context. I also think back to my original understanding of AI in creative work. I still value the human element of creativity and would often prefer something created by a person, even if it is less polished, because the imperfections can reflect individual choices and ideas. I am not against using AI, but I think it is important to recognize the difference between using it to support a creative or research process and allowing it to replace the thinking behind that process.
For now, I would describe my digital literacy as something that extends beyond being comfortable with digital tools. At the beginning of the course, I mainly thought about what technology allowed me to do. Now, I find myself thinking more about how it works, how information is transformed through it, and what might be lost or misrepresented along the way. From analyzing texts to creating datasets from historical documents and exploring how computers interpret images, I have learned that digital methods do not simply present information objectively. They involve choices, assumptions, and limitations that need to be understood. Going forward, I want to continue exploring ways of combining my interests in Economics, Interactive Media, and computational research.

It is also the model I use the most. Gemini and Claude, on the other hand, confidently mentioned things that I have honestly never even heard of or used, and some of the specific projects they described. Claude also stood out because it started more like a story or personal reflection instead of immediately explaining digital literacy. Comparing the three showed me how much the information a model has about the person can affect how accurate and personalized its response becomes.
