# Digital Literacy Narrative	

## ChatGPT
As a 19 year old Economics student at NYU Abu Dhabi with a minor in Interactive Media, I have come to realize that digital literacy is not simply about knowing how to use technology. It is also about understanding how digital tools shape the way we create, communicate, research, and think. Technology is already a major part of my daily and academic life, even when I do not consciously think of what I am doing as “digital literacy.”
For content creation, I regularly use tools such as Canva, CapCut, Google Slides, and social media platforms. Canva in particular has become one of the tools I am most comfortable with because I use it to create presentations, posters, social media content, and other visual materials. CapCut allows me to edit videos and create short form content. I also use platforms such as Instagram, WhatsApp, and email for social interaction and communication. These tools have taught me that digital literacy involves more than knowing which buttons to click. I have to think about how information is presented, who my audience is, what visual choices communicate, and how easily someone can understand what I have created.
Academically, my digital skills are more connected to research, data, and analysis. As an Economics major, I work with quantitative information and am learning to use R and RStudio for data analysis. I have also used Stata, spreadsheets, and online databases for working with economic data. These tools allow me to organize information, manipulate datasets, create graphs, and look for patterns that would be difficult to identify manually. At the same time, I am realizing that being able to manipulate data does not automatically mean that I understand it. Digital literacy also requires me to question where data comes from, how it was collected, what might be missing, and whether the conclusions I draw from it are actually justified.
One thing I find particularly interesting about being in an interdisciplinary classroom is that everyone comes into the room with a different kind of digital knowledge. Someone studying Computer Science may approach a problem through programming and algorithms, while an Interactive Media or Film and New Media student might think more about visual communication, storytelling, and user experience. An Environmental Studies student could bring experience working with geographical or environmental datasets, while someone studying Literature or Creative Writing might approach digital tools through language, interpretation, and storytelling. These differences are valuable because they challenge the assumption that there is only one way to be digitally literate. I may know how to work with economic data, but another student may know how to visualize or communicate that same information in a way I would not have considered.
This is where I think Digital Humanities can offer new possibilities for how I understand my own field. Economics is often presented through numbers, models, graphs, and equations. Digital Humanities makes me think about how computational tools can also be used to study human experiences, culture, language, history, and society. It makes me wonder how economic questions could be explored alongside qualitative information, visual material, or large collections of texts. For example, instead of only looking at statistics about a particular country or population, computational methods could potentially help analyze how people talk about economic issues through thousands of articles, speeches, or social media posts. This could allow me to see relationships between quantitative and qualitative information that I might otherwise overlook.
Collaboration can also help develop my computational thinking. I usually think of computational thinking as something strongly associated with coding or computer science. However, I am beginning to understand it more broadly as breaking a complex problem into smaller parts, identifying patterns, organizing information, and figuring out a systematic way to approach a problem. Working with people from different disciplines could help me recognize approaches that I would not naturally use as an Economics student. Someone else might ask a question about a dataset that I would never think to ask, or approach a problem from a completely different angle.
AI is another area where I am still developing my understanding. I already use AI tools for brainstorming, explaining difficult concepts, and exploring ideas, but I am becoming more aware that using AI effectively requires critical thinking. I am especially interested in the question of where AI should fit into creative and academic work. When AI can generate an entire document, design, or website, I question what happens to individual creativity and effort. I would often rather see something created by a person, even if it is less polished, because the imperfections can reflect the person's own ideas and choices.
Overall, I would describe my current digital literacy as practical but still developing. I am comfortable using many digital tools, but this course is encouraging me to think more critically about what those tools actually do and how they influence the way I understand the world. Going forward, I want to explore new ways of working with data, especially methods that combine computational analysis with creative and human-centered approaches. I also want to become more thoughtful about AI rather than simply seeing it as a tool for making tasks easier. For me, developing digital literacy means learning not only how to use technology, but also when to use it, why to use it, and when a human perspective is more valuable than the technology itself.
## Gemini
As a nineteen-year-old university student at NYU Abu Dhabi majoring in Economics with a minor in Interactive Media (IM), my relationship with digital literacy is double-sided. In economics, numbers are often treated as objective truths, boiled down to clean regressions and market models. In Interactive Media, technology is physical, messier, and deeply human. Living between these two worlds on a campus defined by intense global diversity has made me realize that digital literacy isn't just about knowing how to code or execute software commands—it is a mindset, a translation layer, and a ongoing critical conversation. 
In my everyday academic life, I rely on an ecosystem of software tools tailored to quantitative rigor and creative expression. For research data management, manipulation, and analysis, I regularly move between Stata, R, and Python, using Jupyter Notebooks to clean large datasets, run econometric models, and run regressions. I manage version control for my code using GitHub, while keeping my notes, literature reviews, and citations organized through Notion and Zotero. On the creative and social side, my IM minor has expanded my toolbox beyond standard slide decks. I use p5.js and Processing for generative art and visual data representations, dynamic web tools for interactive storytelling, and Figma for UI/UX prototyping. For content creation and social interaction, platforms like Discord, Notion, and Arena serve as digital drawing boards where I share mood boards, code snippets, and design concepts asynchronously with peers across time zones.
Yet, stepping into an interdisciplinary classroom at NYU Abu Dhabi quickly reveals the vast spectrum of "digital literacy" in the room. Working alongside Computer Science majors means seeing code in terms of systemic efficiency and algorithmic elegance. Collaborating with Film and New Media or Literature students brings an entirely different perspective: they analyze how digital structures carry narrative, emotion, and cultural subtext. Environmental Studies and Engineering students bring spatial, ecological, and physical hardware awareness. Initially, these differences can create friction—an economist wants a chart, a programmer wants an optimized script, and an artist wants an experience.
This intersection is precisely where the Digital Humanities (DH) offer transformative new horizons. DH bridges the gap between raw data and human experience. In economics, we often reduce complex human decisions to static variables ($Y = \beta_0 + \beta_1 X + \epsilon$). Digital humanities forces us to ask: Who is represented in this dataset, who is erased, and what cultural or historical biases shaped these metrics? Applying DH frameworks to economic data transforms flat spreadsheets into interactive, humanized narratives, allowing us to visualize historical trade routes, economic disparities, or migration patterns not just as abstract statistics, but as lived human stories. 
Collaborating across these disciplines actively expands my "computational thinking". Computational thinking is often misconstrued as simply learning to write code; in reality, it is the ability to break down complex problems, identify patterns, abstract key elements, and design algorithmic solutions. When I work with a Literature major on a text-mining project or a Computer Science student on an interactive physical computing build, my approach to problem-solving transforms. I stop viewing technology as a sterile calculator and start viewing it as an extensible medium for thought.
Furthermore, our coursework provides a crucial space to critically evaluate artificial intelligence. Rather than viewing AI tools as magical shortcuts or existential threats, interdisciplinary dialogue encourages us to demystify them. We learn to look under the hood of Machine Learning models, interrogating training sets, algorithmic bias, and the political economy of hardware infrastructure.
Reflecting on my current abilities, I recognize both my strengths and my blind spots. I am comfortable handling quantitative data and building functional prototypes, but I sometimes default to treating data as neutral ground. This course is pushing me to unlearn that assumption. I am discovering that every dataset is an artifact of design, shaped by choices about what to measure and what to ignore.
Moving forward, I want to explore new ways of thinking that bridge economic modeling with generative media and critical data studies. I want to experiment with interactive agent-based simulations that visualize economic theory through physical computing installations or immersive web interfaces. Ultimately, digital literacy is not a static set of technical skills to be checked off a list. It is an evolving fluency—a commitment to remain curious, technically capable, and ethically grounded as we build and interrogate the digital systems shaping our world. 
## Claude
I arrived at this course assuming I was digitally literate. I have been making things on a computer since I was twelve. I can build a clean slide deck, I know my way around a spreadsheet, and there is a p5.js sketch on my laptop that reacts to my webcam. Three weeks in, I have started to suspect that being fluent with tools and being literate about them are different capacities, and that I had quietly substituted one for the other.
My daily practice is more scattered than I realised until I wrote it down. For Interactive Media I work in p5.js and Figma, wire up Arduino sensors in the lab, and cut footage in CapCut or Premiere when a project needs documentation. For Economics I draft in Overleaf because LaTeX makes my regression tables look like they belong in a paper, even when the analysis behind them is shaky. I run the Instagram for a student club, which means I think about aspect ratios, captions, and posting times more seriously than I think about my own citations. Socially, my life is spread across WhatsApp groups that never sleep, a Discord server where we crowd-solve problem sets, shared Google Docs edited by four people at 2am, and LinkedIn, which I treat as a performance rather than a conversation.
My research workflow is where I have the most to fix. I use Zotero connected through the NYU library proxy, Google Scholar alerts for a few authors I follow, and JSTOR when a professor mentions something older. For data I pull from FRED, the World Bank's open data portal, IMF statistics, and Our World in Data. Analysis happens in Stata for econometrics, R with tidyverse and ggplot2 when I want control over a plot, and increasingly pandas inside a Jupyter notebook. I have used OpenRefine once, on a truly filthy CSV, and it felt like cheating. I know Git exists. I do not really use it. My folders are full of files called final_v3_ACTUALFINAL.
What has surprised me most is the room itself. Sitting around one table are people who notice completely different things first. My Computer Science classmates immediately ask how the data is structured. The Film and New Media students ask who is holding the camera and what got left out of the frame. Environmental Studies people open QGIS without being asked and start layering satellite imagery. The engineers reach for MATLAB and think in systems and tolerances. And the Literature and Creative Writing students ask the question that stops me every time: whose voice is this, and what is it trying to make me feel? I had been trained to treat a dataset as a given. They treat it as a text.
That is where digital humanities has opened something up for me. Economics already does text-as-data without always calling it that, running sentiment analysis on central bank minutes or topic models over policy documents. But approaching a corpus the way a digital humanist does, asking about provenance, silences, and who did the labour of classification, reframes my own discipline. A World Bank indicator is not a fact of nature. It is the residue of decisions made by people with budgets and deadlines. Distant reading, network analysis of trade relationships, mapping as argument rather than decoration: these feel less like new tools and more like new questions.
Collaboration has been the fastest route into what this course calls computational thinking. I learned more about decomposition from pair-debugging a friend's broken loop at midnight than from any lecture on algorithms. Watching someone else break a problem into pieces, name the pieces, and test them one at a time is a transferable habit, and it is starting to show up in how I structure an essay.
AI is where my critical reflection gets uncomfortable. I use it constantly, often lazily, as autocomplete and as a rubber duck. It is very good at producing fluent output, and fluency is exactly what I am worst at resisting. I want to move from being a consumer of models to someone who can interrogate one: what it was trained on, whose labour annotated it, what it costs in energy and water in a region where water is not abstract.
